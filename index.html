Welcome.

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at Virginia Tech. My research is in microeconomic theory, particularly networks and game theory.

I study how people make decisions when they know their own connections but have limited information about the broader network. I examine how their beliefs shape behavior, what they learn from repeated interactions, and how limited information affects policy interventions.

In my Job Market Paper, I study a policymaker who removes one person from a network to reduce overall activity. Consider the police arresting one member of a criminal network to reduce crime. Police operations are kept secret, so the arrest comes as a surprise and is never announced. Only the arrested member’s direct associates notice the absence, because they dealt with that member directly. Each associate knows their own contacts but not the rest of the network. They must therefore form beliefs about whether their other associates also know of the arrest, and they adjust their behavior based on those beliefs. The arrest therefore reduces crime in two ways. The arrested member’s own activity is lost, and the associates who learn of the arrest change their activity. This second effect depends on what the associates believe others know, and it can change whom the policymaker should target. Effective intervention thus depends on who is connected to whom and also on who learns about the intervention.

At Virginia Tech, I have taught undergraduate Principles of Microeconomics and Microeconomic Theory, as well as Math Camp for incoming PhD students. I received the department’s Graduate Student Instructor of the Year award in 2025. For my Principles course, I co-developed Marginal Falls Economic Quests, a collection of interactive online games that students play together in class. Each game places students in the fictional town of Marginal Falls where they make economic choices, and the class then discusses the economics behind those choices.
